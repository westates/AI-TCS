I. Overview/Background 
The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) is a federal law that prohibits discrimination against people with 
disabilities in everyday activities.1 The ADA contains specific requirements for state and local governments to 
ensure equal access for people with disabilities. The law requires accommodation for pedestrians with 
disabilities not only in completed, publicly accessible facilities but also during times of construction or 
improvement. Another federal law, the Architectural Barriers Act (ABA), requires public agencies to provide 
access to facilities designed, built, altered, or leased with Federal funds.
2 Sidewalks are considered a facility 
under federal law.   
When temporary traffic control (TTC) zones affect the movement of pedestrians, adequate pedestrian access 
and walkways shall be provided (Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) (11th Edition, Dec. 
2023) Section 6C.02 Pedestrian Considerations, Standard 03). All pedestrians, including the disabled, should be 
provided with safe, accessible, and reasonably convenient paths that replicate, as nearly as practical, the most 
desirable characteristics of the existing pedestrian circulation facilities when TTC zones are planned and TPAR 
is set up (MUTCD Section 6C.02 Pedestrian Considerations, Guidance 07). 
This document, Temporary Pedestrian Accessible Routes (TPAR) Manual: Access for People with Disabilities 
is designed for project managers, designers, contractors, utility companies, tree trimming or painting 
companies, field inspectors, and anyone else involved with the design or implementation of TPAR. The intent 
of this manual is to describe TPAR requirements, methods, and best practices to ensure accessibility for 
people with disabilities in work zones, including those who are blind or have low vision.   
For purposes here, the definition of work in public rights-of-way refers to alterations of existing elements, new 
construction, maintenance, repairs, or similar that cause interruptions in the continuous unobstructed path 
connecting accessible elements and spaces. Work zones are areas of a roadway or travel way where 
construction, maintenance, or utility work activities take place. A work zone extends between the first warning 
sign to the last TTC device. 
The needs of pedestrians with disabilities must be addressed in temporary traffic control plans (TCPs) in 
accordance with the ADA. Designers, planners, and contractors creating and implementing TCPs have two 
primary considerations: design for the built environment and communications.   
1. Design for the built environment: physical devices, measurable components such as distances and 
slopes and their established design parameters, and maintaining access (i.e. TPAR connectivity) to 
businesses, transit, and other nearby destinations. The TPAR must provide a safe detour for all users 
around the work zone. Worksite personnel must ensure that the TPAR is adequately maintained and 
pedestrian walkways are kept clear throughout the duration of a project. 
2. Communications: Rules issued by the US Department of Justice under the ADA require local 
governments to ensure that communications with individuals with disabilities are as effective as 
communications with others.

TPAR Considerations for People with Disabilities 
Engineers, architects, contractors, and building officials must keep in mind pedestrians with disabilities, 
including those with vision and mobility disabilities. According to 2024 data from the Centers for Disease 
Control and Prevention (CDC), more than 1 in 4 adults in the US (28.7%) have some type of disability.5 Around 
30% of the US population does not drive.6 People with disabilities live, work, and navigate throughout our 
communities every day. Many disabilities can impact how a pedestrian may be able to navigate a work zone 
safely.   
Some people may have more than one disability, which may or may not be apparent to others. For example, a 
person who is blind or has low vision may also have a hearing impairment, mobility impairment, or cognitive 
impairment. Since people with different disabilities have different needs, circumstances on a work site often 
must rely on judgment. 
Consideration for People with Vision Disabilities 
According to CDC data from 2024, 5.5% of adults in the US have a vision disability, described by the CDC as 
blindness or serious difficulty seeing.7 People who are blind or have low vision are not a homogeneous group. 
There is significant variation across individuals regarding their degree and type of vision loss.   
Safety Concerns for People with Vision Disabilities 
A small percentage of people with vision disabilities have guide dogs that lead them away from or around 
hazards.8 Those without guide dogs must find their own way, whether or not there is a safe route for them to 
use. When a person with a vision disability finds a safe path, they may use it exclusively to remain safe while 
traveling, which makes changes caused by work zones a safety risk. When work zones do not provide TPAR, 
pedestrians may travel into the roadway which can be dangerous for any pedestrian, especially those with 
vision disabilities.   
• Common TPAR wayfinding challenges and safety issues for pedestrians with vision disabilities are: 
• Difficulty recognizing that they have come upon a work zone.   
• Not knowing if there is a safe alternative way to proceed when a sidewalk is closed.

Consider this scenario: A person who is blind is on their way to a job interview. They encounter caution tape 
and cones within their normal path of travel. They ponder where to go to get to their transit stop. Unsure 
where to go and finding no one to ask, they return home and use rideshare to assure they do not miss their 
interview. They arrive only a few minutes late for their interview but are very stressed.   
Consider the same scenario but with a different experience: Instead of floundering on caution tape and 
cones, the TPAR is communicated via audible messages. The TPAR also uses a channelizing barrier that a white 
cane can detect to move them into and out of the street, with compliant temporary ramps and detectable 
warning surfaces. The person can continue to their transit stop in plenty of time to catch the bus, arriving at 
their interview relaxed and a few minutes early, as planned.   
In both cases the individual did make their interview, but the first scenario does not communicate that a work 
zone is ahead nor provide a channelized pedestrian access route to allow them to move around the hazardous 
work zone. Without the ability to see a detour at a work zone, pedestrians with vision disabilities have 
significant disadvantages in comparison to the general public.   
Design Considerations for People with Vision Disabilities 
In designing accessible routes, it is important to consider the needs of pedestrians with vision disabilities to 
ensure everyone is kept as safe as possible. People with vision disabilities need a physical environment that is 
free of sharp edges, uneven grading, obstructions that can cause injury, and vertical or horizontal changes in 
the walking surface that can cause tripping or falling hazards.   
Those with vision disabilities may not be able to recognize where the street begins when curbs are flush with 
the sidewalks. Detectable warning surfaces are tactile material installed before the flush curb in a blended 
transition or curb ramp. Detectable warning surfaces consist of truncated domes in a square or radial grid 
pattern (Public Right-of-Way Accessibility Guidelines (PROWAG) R305.1 General). At the boundary between 
the sidewalk and vehicle travel lane, detectable warning surfaces can be felt by a person navigating with a 
white cane or underfoot by a person with a dog guide.

Some people with low vision may depend on colors as a warning. Detectable warning surfaces shall visually

Communicating a TPAR for People with Vision Disabilities 
A city can ensure accessibility by improving communication at the site of the work zone. Visual signs are a 
typical part of TPAR but are not usable by pedestrians with vision disabilities. Blocked routes, alternate 
crossings, and sign and signal information should be communicated to pedestrians with vision disabilities by 
providing audible information, tactile and/or vibrating surface devices, accessible pedestrian signal walk 
indications, and barriers and channelizing devices with continuous detectable edging (PROWAG R303.2 Signs, 
R307 Pedestrian Push Buttons and Passive Pedestrian Detection, R308 Accessible Pedestrian Signal Walk 
Indications, R303.6 Detectable Edging of Channelizing Devices, and MUTCD Section 6C.03 Accessibility 
Considerations, Guidance 06).   
Audible information devices (AIDs) described in the PROWAG indicates that signage that directs pedestrians to 
and from detours shall be provided via proximity-actuated (vs. button push) audible signs or other non-visual 
format so that it is accessible to people with vision disabilities (PROWAG R303.2 Signs). Commercially available 
AIDs can provide the required audible signs using infrared or other motion sensors. Since AIDs are known to 
be susceptible to theft, the devices should be secured to the traffic control device (barricade, channelization, 
fence, etc.).   
The audible message can be customized so the devices can be re-used for multiple construction projects over 
time. The audible message should be brief, to the point, spoken clearly, and recorded in a quiet area. 
Incorporate directions that do not require visual recognition. Message example: “Attention eastbound 
Washington Avenue pedestrians. You are at the southeast corner of Washington and Church. Road 
construction on Washington from Church St to Huron for 7 blocks. Protected pedestrian path open on this 
side. Work will continue until August 20.”

Consideration for People with Mobility Disabilities 
According to CDC data from 2024, 12.2% of adults in the US have a mobility disability, described by the CDC as 
serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs.10 People with mobility disabilities may use canes, crutches, 
walkers, wheelchairs, scooters, or no assistive devices at all. Respiratory, circulatory, and neurological medical 
conditions can cause mobility disabilities that most people will not notice. Field personnel in work zones 
should not rely on visual cues that someone might have a disability, and should expect to always maintain 
TPAR, as legally required. 
Safety Concerns for People with Mobility Disabilities 
Common safety issues for pedestrians with mobility disabilities are: 
• Temporary ramps that are too steep, wobble, or do not have an adequate side guard rail to grasp. 
• Pathways and turns or turning spaces that are too narrow for wheelchairs, motorized scooters, or 
those who travel with service animals. 
• Ramps or pathways that lack adequate edge protection along the outer edges, which during wet 
weather can allow mud or debris to flow over the surface of the ramp or path, creating slippery 
conditions that can be very dangerous to those who use crutches, canes, or walkers. 
Design Considerations for People with Mobility Disabilities 
To ensure accessibility at work zones, it is important to consider the needs of pedestrians with mobility 
disabilities. A person with a mobility disability may have difficulty using steps or vertical curbs, or navigating 
narrow pathways, turnarounds, severe slopes, or changes in elevation. These components of the work zone 
can prevent travel, create tripping hazards, or cause individuals to fall out of their mobility assistance devices. 
Even small elevation changes are sometimes difficult for people with mobility disabilities to traverse, which is 
one reason that installing ramps is essential to maintaining access. 
People using wheelchairs or other mobility devices rely on flush curbs or transitions to safely continue travel. 
For a pedestrian with a mobility disability, a vertical barrier acts like a wheel stop does for a vehicle. 
Detectable warning surfaces that consist of truncated domes in a square or radial grid pattern allow wheeled 
devices to travel in the flat troughs between them. For accessible TPAR design, the needs of pedestrians with 
mobility disabilities must be considered.

III. Defined Terms   
The terms used in this document have been drawn from the applicable sources. Where necessary, additional 
commentary has been added for improved clarity. 
Accessible: A pedestrian facility or element in the public right-of-way that complies with the PROWAG 
(PROWAG R104.3 Defined Terms: A). Commentary: The combination of various elements in a facility, site, or 
area, or portion thereof which allows access, circulation, and the full use of the facilities by people with 
disabilities. 
Audible Information Device (AID, Audible Signage): An audible information device (AID) is a device designed 
to provide information to pedestrians with vision disabilities. It delivers audible messages equivalent to visual 
signage for tasks such as notifying pedestrians about sidewalk closures and detours. Commentary: the 
PROWAG refers to AIDs as proximity actuated audible signs (PROWAG R303.2 Signs). 
Blended Transition: Blended transitions are raised pedestrian street crossings, depressed corners, or 
similar connections between pedestrian access routes at the level of the sidewalk and the level of the 
pedestrian street crossing that have a grade of 5% or less. Blended transitions are suitable for a range of 
sidewalk conditions. 
Cross Slope: The slope that is perpendicular to the direction of pedestrian travel (PROWAG R104.3 Defined 
Terms: C). Commentary: The slope of a walking surface is regulated in the two directions of travel (see running 
slope). Cross slope makes forward travel for people using wheelchairs increasingly difficult. As the cross slope 
increases, travel becomes increasingly difficult as a wheelchair or walker is pulled in the direction of the slope, 
risking tipping over and potential injury to themselves or others in their path. Cross slope impedes travel on 
uphill and downhill slopes by pedestrians who use wheeled mobility devices, braces, lower limb prostheses, 
crutches, or walkers, as well as pedestrians who have gait, balance, or stamina impairments.

Curb Line: A line at the face of the curb that marks the transition between the curb and the gutter or street 
(PROWAG R104.3 Defined Terms: C). 
Curb Ramp: A sloped connection that is cut through or built up to a curb. Curb ramps may be perpendicular or 
parallel to the curb or to the street they serve or be a combination thereof (PROWAG R104.3 Defined Terms: 
C). Perpendicular curb ramps have a running slope that is perpendicular to the curb or the street it serves, and 
parallel curb ramps have a running slope parallel to the curb or street it serves. (See PROWAG R304 Curb 
Ramps and Blended Transitions for additional requirements.) 
Detectable: Having a continuous edge within 6 inches of the surface so that pedestrians with vision disabilities 
can sense its presence and receive usable guidance information (MUTCD Section 1C.02 Definitions of Words 
and Phrases Used in this Manual, No. 58).   
Detectable Warning Surface: A standardized surface feature built in or applied to pedestrian circulation paths 
and other pedestrian facilities to warn of hazards (PROWAG R104.3 Defined Terms: D). Detectable warning 
surfaces mark boundaries between pedestrian and vehicular ways where there is no raised curb. Detectable 
warning surfaces are installed where curb ramps are constructed at the junction of pedestrian access routes 
and the roadway or shoulder, for marked and unmarked crosswalks. Detectable warning surfaces contrast 
visually with adjacent walking surfaces, either light-on-dark or dark-on-light (MUTCD Section 3C.01 General 
Crosswalk Markings, Support 04). Detectable warning surfaces shall consist of truncated domes in a square or 
radial grid pattern (PROWAG R305.1 General). Commentary: Truncated domes mark the junction where 
pedestrians travel into and out of the roadway. 
Disability: With respect to an individual— a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or 
more major life activities of such individual; a record of such an impairment; or being regarded as having such 
an impairment.15 Commentary: In the context of the ADA, disability is a legal definition and may vary from 
other medical or social justice-related definitions.   
Diversion or Detour: Alternate pedestrian path of travel around a work zone. (See Temporary Pedestrian 
Accessible Route.) 
Guide Dog (Dog Guide): A guide dog responds to the handler's verbal and/or hand commands (e.g., right, left, 
forward, hop up, etc.). Guide dogs do not make decisions about the route or decide when to cross a street— 
these decisions are made by the person they are serving.   
Mobility Disability (Physical Disability): Mobility impairment refers to the inability of a person to use one or 
more of their extremities, or a lack of strength to walk, grasp, or lift objects. A wheelchair, crutches, or a 
walker may be utilized to aid in mobility. Mobility impairment may be caused by a number of factors, such as 
disease, an accident, or a congenital disorder and may be the result from neuro-muscular and orthopedic 
impairments.

Pedestrian: A person on foot, travelling by wheelchair or other mobility device, on skates, or on a skateboard.

Pedestrian Access Route (PAR): An accessible, continuous, and unobstructed path of travel for use by 
pedestrians with disabilities within a pedestrian circulation path (PROWAG R104.3 Defined Terms: P). 
Commentary: The PAR is an accessible route in public rights-of-way, or a continuous unobstructed accessible 
route connecting accessible elements and spaces that can be negotiated by people with a disability using a 
wheelchair, and that are also safe for and usable by people with other disabilities such as blindness or low 
vision. PARs may include parking access aisles, curb ramps, crosswalks at vehicular ways, walks, ramps, and 
lifts with the intent to remain usable for people with disabilities during construction, repair, or maintenance 
work.   
Pedestrian Channelizing Device (PCD): Pedestrian channelizing devices may be used to close a PAR or indicate 
a suitable path for pedestrian travel around or through the work zone. Devices used to channelize pedestrians 
shall be detectable to users of white canes and visible to pedestrians with vision disabilities. When used as a 
sidewalk closure, the device shall cover the entire width of the sidewalk (MUTCD Section 6K.02 Pedestrian 
Channelizing Devices, Standard 06-07). The MUTCD allows several other devices such as longitudinal 
channelizing devices and temporary traffic barriers to be used as PCDs under certain conditions. Additionally, 
the PROWAG and MUTCD provide information on the requirements related to detectable edging of 
channelizing devices. 
Pedestrian Circulation Path: A prepared exterior or interior surface provided for pedestrian use in the public 
right-of-way (PROWAG R104.3 Defined Terms: P). Commentary: A PAR is a subset of the pedestrian circulation 
route. In an existing facility constructed prior to the effective date of the ADA, the circulation route may not 
be accessible in its entirety, but the PAR must be accessible. 
Pedestrian Facility: A structure, route, or space for pedestrian circulation or use located in the public right-of-
way (PROWAG R104.3 Defined Terms: P). 
Pedestrian Hybrid Beacon (PHB): A special type of hybrid beacon used to warn and control traffic at an 
unsignalized location to assist pedestrians in crossing a street at a marked crosswalk (PROWAG R104.3 Defined 
Terms: P). PHBs are intentionally placed in a dark mode (no indications displayed) between periods of 
operation and, when operated, the device displays both steady and flashing traffic control signal indications 
(MUTCD Section 1C.02 Definitions of Words and Phrases Used in this Manual, No. 105). Commentary: A traffic 
control device can be used to increase awareness for motorists of pedestrian crossings at uncontrolled marked 
crosswalk locations. A PHB is only activated by pedestrians when needed.

highly mobile barrier, movable and temporary steel barrier, movable concrete barrier, traditional concrete

Running Slope: The slope that is parallel to the direction of pedestrian travel (PROWAG R104.3 Defined Terms: 
R). Running slope is referred to as longitudinal or running slope. The slope percentage determines whether a 
walking surface is a sloped walkway or a ramp regulated under the ADA and the PROWAG. Running slope in a 
pedestrian facility is further defined by location in reference to the roadway. 
Temporary: Structures or elements intended for use for a limited amount of time during work. State and 
federal accessibility standards apply to temporary and permanent structures (see 2010 ADA Standards for 
Accessible Design (ADAS) 201.3 Temporary and Permanent Structures).   
Temporary Curb Ramp (Temporary Ramp): See requirements for Curb Ramps. 
Temporary Pedestrian Accessible Route (TPAR): (See PAR for definition of pedestrian access route.) A TPAR is 
path of travel, marked by signing, delineation, and traffic control devices, used by pedestrians to navigate 
through or around work zones. The TPAR is included as part of the Traffic Control Plan. A TPAR is required 
when a PAR is closed due to construction, maintenance operations, or other similar conditions. 
Commentary: The PROWAG and MUTCD refer to TPAR as alternate pedestrian access routes. Both the 
PROWAG and MUTCD require TPAR to be as accessible as the existing PAR. However, the PROWAG permits an 
alternate means of providing access for pedestrians with disabilities, if establishing or maintaining a TPAR is

zone (MUTCD Section 6C.02 Pedestrian Considerations, Option 04-05). The PROWAG includes more specific 
requirements for alternate pedestrian access routes, describing the required level of detail on sidewalk 
closures and rerouting. In many cases, compliance with the requirements for TPAR will not entail any change 
from baseline conditions because they closely align with existing MUTCD provisions on temporary traffic 
control. Additional PROWAG and MUTCD guidance includes the following elements: 
• Signs to warn pedestrians of an alternate route in advance to indicate location of detour (MUTCD 
Section 6I.02 Detour Signs and Plaques and PROWAG 303.2 Signs). 
• A smooth, continuous alternate pedestrian route, including curb ramps (or an absence of curbs) 
(MUTCD Section 6C.02 Pedestrian Considerations, Guidance 11(C)) and PROWAG R303.5 Curb Ramp or 
Blended Transition). 
• Accessible pedestrian signals, AIDs, or detectable barriers and channelizing devices to communicate 
signal information to travelers with low vision (see MUTCD Chapter 4K Accessible Pedestrian Signals 
and Detectors, PROWAG R303.7 Pedestrian Signal Heads, R303.2 Signs, and R303.6 Detectable Edging 
of Channelizing Devices).

IV. TPAR Design Elements & Considerations 
Devices and Features 
TPAR devices and features that are required to provide an accessible alternate pedestrian pathway are similar 
to those required to guide traffic through a work zone. As with drivers, pedestrians must be warned in 
advance of changing conditions and advised of their options for alternate routes with information that they 
can access. When they approach the work area, pedestrians with disabilities need guidance on any changes 
from the normal route, such as shifts in the walkway alignment, in the same way that drivers must be warned 
about lane changes. As with vehicles, when pedestrians traverse the work zone, the route must be clearly 
marked, have a suitable surface, and have protection from other traffic and work operations, including drop-
offs. When pedestrians exit the work zone, they need to be guided back to their original route.   
Clear Width 
Pedestrians with wheelchairs and other mobility assistance devices need TPAR to be at least 60 inches wide so 
that two people can pass by each other if they meet head on. If a 60-inch continuous path cannot be created 
over the entire length, a minimum 48-inch walkway with a 60-by-60-inch passing space every 200 feet 
maximum shall be provided (see MUTCD Section 6C.02 Pedestrian Considerations, Guidance 11(D)).   
Signs and Surface Delineators 
Printed signs and vertical surface delineators (cones or tubular markers) are not usable by pedestrians with 
vision disabilities. Therefore, blocked routes, alternate crossings, and sign and signal information should be 
communicated to pedestrians with vision disabilities by providing audible information devices, tactile and/or 
vibrating surface devices, and barriers and channelizing devices that are detectable to pedestrians traveling 
with the aid of a white cane or who have vision disabilities (MUTCD Section 6C.03 Accessibility Considerations, 
Guidance 06). Technology today allows the provision of information to pedestrians with vision disabilities that 
is equivalent to visual signing for notification of sidewalk closures in a speech message provided by an AID. 
Devices that provide speech messages in response to passive pedestrian actuation are the most desirable, as 
they do not require actuation.   
Walking Surfaces 
Surface conditions are required to be firm, stable, and slip-resistant (ADAS 302.1 General and PROWAG R302.6 
Surfaces). In practical terms, paths of travel should be clear of debris and adequately drained to prevent the 
accumulation of trash or mud that can cause a tripping or slipping hazard. The TPAR should be able to support 
a wheelchair under all conditions (dry, rain, snow, etc.). The TPAR shall be checked daily for any standing 
water or mud to ensure debris such as sand, dirt, or leaves are not creating a slippery walking surface. 
Source: lhblind.org

The surface of the pedestrian path should be as clear and as flat as possible. Many pedestrians with mobility 
disabilities cannot navigate steps or uneven surfaces without becoming disoriented or losing their balance.   
The 2010 ADAS require horizontal joints or other openings to be no more than 1⁄2 inch wide or flush to prevent 
tripping and to reduce the possibility of canes or small wheels getting trapped in gaps or open spaces. If 
drainage grate openings are located within the pedestrian route, openings must be perpendicular to the 
pathway and must be narrow enough that a sphere greater than 1⁄2 inch in diameter will not pass through (see 
ADAS 302.3 Openings and 403.2 Floor or Ground Surface). Sidewalk ramp surfaces shall have a running slope 
not to exceed 8.3% and cross slope not to exceed 2.1% (PROWAG R304.2.1 Running Slope and R304.2.2 Cross 
Slope). Gutter surfaces in the roadway can be problematic when the alternate route is required to be at least 
as accessible as the existing path that was previously available to pedestrians. Engineering judgment must be 
used.   
Requirements for detectable edging are dependent on the location. Where a channelizing device is used, 
detectable edging shall be located along the walking surface with openings no higher than 2 inches from the 
ground with a top edge at least 32 inches high and free of sharp edges (see PROWAG R303.6.1 Top and 
R303.6.2 Bottom). Detectable pedestrian edging should be orange, white, or yellow and should match the 
color of the adjacent channelizing or traffic control devices, when present.   
Temporary Curb Ramps 
When pedestrians are diverted to a TPAR in the roadway and a vertical curb separates the roadway from the 
sidewalk, temporary curb ramps must be provided to enable pedestrians to safely travel around the work 
zone. Temporary curb ramps can be constructed out of a variety of materials including asphalt, wood, and 
synthetic pre-made systems. Each ramp must be regularly maintained to ensure usability for the life of the 
ramp’s use.   
Temporary curb ramps shall be the full width of the temporary route, with a recommended 60-inch width and 
a minimum width of 48 inches (see PROWAG R303.4 Continuous Clear Width).   
The PROWAG limits running slope (slope in the direction of travel) for all ramps at 1:12 or 8.3% and cross slope 
(slope perpendicular to travel) at 1:48 or 2.1% (PROWAG R304.2.1 Running Slope and R304.2.2 Cross Slope). 
Detectable warning surfaces shall be provided in accordance with the PROWAG (PROWAG R304.1 General and 
R205 Detectable Warning Surfaces). If the temporary ramp provides access to an enclosed safety corridor for 
passage along the construction zone, accessible truncated domes must be provided at the location where a 
street crossing occurs. If the temporary ramps do not lead to an area where pedestrians may conjoin the 
vehicular traffic lane or bike lane, detectable warnings may not be required. These situations are typical where 
construction is taking place in smaller patch-work areas along sidewalks and pedestrians are diverted into the 
roadway in a completely protected area with two curb ramps providing access.   
Some agencies have established specific requirements for the design of temporary curb ramps to 
accommodate pedestrians with mobility disabilities. Many private vendors now manufacture and sell 
temporary curb ramps for use at vertical curbs and ramps for use to access entrances at businesses. These 
elements are durable and adjustable to conditions on-site. These elements can be moved from construction 
site to site and may be more cost effective than other options.   
Crosswalks in TPAR 
Cross slopes at crosswalks are regulated based on intersection control or where it is located, such as at 
roundabouts or midblock crossings. The cross slope at crosswalks that are not controlled or that are controlled

the roadway up to a maximum of 5% (PROWAG R302.5.2.2 Crosswalk at Uncontrolled Approach and 
R302.5.2.3 Crosswalk with Traffic Control Signal or Pedestrian Hybrid Beacon). The cross slope at intersections

Lighting   
Lighting is important to all pedestrians, not only enabling them to see signs and instructions clearly but also to 
make pedestrians visible to automotive traffic, bike traffic, and other pedestrians. Consideration should be 
given to avoid trees and other objects that would cast a shadow and obstruct the light output. A TPAR is 
required to remain day and night with existing barriers and signs in place to protect and inform pedestrians 
(see MUTCD Section 2A.21 Retroreflection and Illumination, Standard 03-05 and Section 6F.01 General 
Characteristics of TTC Zone Signs, Standard 11-12). 
Detectable Edging 
A pedestrian with a vision disability experiences physical barriers daily. Their safety depends on knowing their 
intended path of travel and not diverting from it, unless they are forced to do so. Signs, drums, cones, caution 
tape, bollards, or typical type I, II, or III barricades themselves do not provide sufficient guidance to 
pedestrians with vision disabilities.   
Where a channelizing device is used to delineate an TPAR, continuous detectable edging shall be provided 
throughout the length of the route. Where pedestrians or vehicles turn or cross, gaps in the detectable edging 
are permitted (PROWAG R303.6 Detectable Edging of Channelizing Devices). These barricades must be at least 
32 inches high and have a bottom edge that is continuous, no higher than 2 inches from the walking surface, 
and detectable by a white cane (see PROWAG Figure R303.6 below).    
Joints between channelizing devices should be closed and flush to prevent canes or small wheels from being 
trapped, to reduce the risk of tripping, and should be free of sharp or rough edges or objects that may harm 
hands, arms, or clothing. Furthermore, when used as a sidewalk closure mechanism, channelizing devices 
must run the entire width of the sidewalk; gaps in coverage could lead to people with vision disabilities 
accidentally crossing into the work zone, where they may be exposed to trips, falls, and severe injuries. The

Physical Separation 
TPAR plans should be designed to minimize conflicts between vehicular and pedestrian movements. 
Consideration should be made to separate pedestrian movements from worksite activity and vehicular traffic. 
Unless an acceptable route that does not involve crossing the roadway can be provided, pedestrians should be 
appropriately directed with advance signing that encourages them to cross to the opposite side of the 
roadway. In areas with high vehicular traffic volumes, signs should be placed at intersections (rather than 
midblock locations) so that pedestrians are not confronted with midblock worksites that could induce them to 
attempt to navigate through the worksite or make a midblock crossing (MUTCD Section 6C.02 Pedestrian 
Considerations, Guidance 09). Where possible, positive physical separation should be used when pedestrians 
are moved off the sidewalk and TPAR walkways are provided in parking areas, bicycle lanes, or the roadway. 
The positive separation between vehicles and pedestrians that will be used should be specified in the written 
TPAR plan.   
Safe TPAR walkways can use different materials based on traffic volume. Higher-volume or higher-speed 
roadways will require more intensive, crash rated, separation devices such as water filled longitudinal barriers, 
concrete barriers, etc., whereas longitudinal channelizing devices may be acceptable on lower speed and 
volume roadways. In any case, drums, bollards, caution tape and cones, or similar will not provide adequate 
separation or continuous cane detectable warning for pedestrians traveling through a work zone adjacent to a 
roadway, drive lane, or parking area (see PROWAG R303.6 Detectable Edging of Channelizing Devices). 
Color contrast is important for pedestrians with low vision and should be considered when selecting 
channelizing devices. Orange is the standard color per the MUTCD for TTC as it provides adequate color

V. TPAR Implementation   
Each work zone is different and will require TPAR that suits the needs of the conditions on site. Common 
issues that adversely affect pedestrians at or near work zones can be found in each type of work zone, 
although the solution may vary depending on several factors. When considering the type of TPAR to best suit 
the needs of pedestrians, three common sets of conditions exist where pedestrian modifications at work 
zones are necessary: 
1. TPAR within Work Zone   
2. TPAR Diverted Around a Work Zone 
3. TPAR Detour/Diversion   
Pedestrian travel must be a consideration when planning the staging of construction phases to ensure that 
pedestrians can travel safely, especially where more than one parallel path of travel exists. Both paths of 
travel should not be closed at the same time, unless an incident such as an emergency exists. With proper 
planning, at least one path of travel serving the public should remain open. 
1. TPAR within Work Zone 
One common situation that pedestrians encounter is work zones 
located in the roadway or on property immediately adjacent to a path 
of travel or sidewalk. Even where the actual path of travel or sidewalk 
is not being worked on or is not formally closed, work zones adjacent 
to the pedestrian path can present hazards and impediments to 
pedestrians. When doing this type of work, field personnel must take 
measures to safeguard pedestrians. Caution tape and wooden stakes 
are not cane detectable warnings that the blind or visually impaired 
can recognize. 
Protection for Pedestrians at Work Zones Adjacent to the Path 
of Travel or Sidewalk 
In low volume, low speed residential areas it may be possible for the 
sidewalk to remain open adjacent to a work zone. If there is not 48 inches of clearance adjacent to the work 
zone, detectable and continuous edge protection must be provided to allow pedestrians with a vision 
disability to safely navigate work zones.   Even with sufficient clearance, pedestrians should be protected from 
excavations and unexpected vertical drop-offs using proper protection and channelization devices. Continuous 
longitudinal channelizers and properly constructed pedestrian barricades can safely separate pedestrians from 
work zones and provide positive path guidance for the blind and visually impaired.

Pedestrian Protection – Work Zone Safety 
If hazards exist within the work zone that require safety precautions for workers (trenches, fall protection, 
hard hats, crane operations, etc.), protection must be provided to ensure pedestrians do not accidentally or 
purposely walk into and across the work zone.   
Continuous concrete barriers, plastic longitudinal channelizing devices, 
wooden enclosed walkways, or cyclone fencing with a continuous, smooth 
edge provided at the bottom can be used for this purpose. A cane detectable 
warning is required along these elements that does not create a hazard for 
other pedestrians. For instance, the bottom edge of the fence serves the blind 
as a cane detectable guide for safe travel, but when the footing protrudes into 
the walking surface, it becomes a trip hazard for other pedestrians (see photo). 
These types of protrusions and abrupt changes must be avoided to ensure all 
pedestrians have a safe and usable walking surface to travel along.   
Pedestrian and Motorist Lines of Sight 
Devices used to prohibit pedestrian access or provide protection from hazards can impede pedestrians and 
motorists from seeing each other or important traffic control devices if not designed or installed properly. 
Similarly, the improper positioning of TTC devices for motorists may also impede their ability to see 
pedestrians and should be moved. The location of work vehicles, equipment, and materials should be 
monitored to maintain maximum visibility.   
End of Shift – Obstacles 
At all times, the pedestrian path of travel or TPAR must be left clear of mud or dirt, poles, temporary signs, 
hoses, barriers, construction materials, vehicles, equipment, and other protruding objects such as tree limbs 
that are part of the work zone and could present hazards to any pedestrian. The TPAR should be reviewed as 
part of this process daily to ensure it remains in place and functions as intended. 
Objects with leading edges more than 27 inches and less than 80 inches above the walking surface shall not 
protrude horizontally more than 4 inches into pedestrian circulation paths. Handrails shall be permitted to 
protrude 4 1⁄2 inches maximum (PROWAG R402.2 Protrusion Limits). Where objects are mounted on a single 
post or pylon and the objects are more than 27 inches and less than 80 inches above the walking surface, the 
objects shall not protrude into the pedestrian circulation path more than 4 inches measured horizontally from 
the post or pylon or more than 4 inches measured horizontally from the outside edge of the base where the 
base height is 2 1⁄2 inches minimum (PROWAG R402.3.1 Objects Mounted on Single Post or Pylon). 
Head room minimum height must also be maintained throughout the TPAR. Where the TPAR uses a canopied 
walkway to protect pedestrians from falling debris or signs are installed within the pedestrian path, the 
MUTCD states that such a walkway or sign should provide at least 7 feet (84 inches) of headroom (see MUTCD 
Section 6F.02 Sign Placement, Standard 05-06), although the ADAS and the PROWAG require only 80 inches 
(PROWAG R402.4 Vertical Clearance and ADAS 307.4 Vertical Clearance).
